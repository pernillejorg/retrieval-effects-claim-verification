## Step 10: Real-World Application (Seafood and Sustainability Claims)

This document records Step 10, the real-world case study. Everything before this point was biomedical: SciFact and SciFact-Open are both scientific claim datasets in the same broad domain, so Steps 1 to 9 could test what happens as retrieval gets harder, but not what happens when the subject domain changes. Step 10 is the one step that changes the domain. It takes 30 real, manually labelled claims about seafood and sustainability, collected from public social media posts, and runs them through the project pipeline against the SciFact-Open corpus. The question is whether the findings from the earlier steps transfer to a domain the pipeline was never built for. Poor retrieval here is expected, because a biomedical corpus mostly has no evidence for seafood claims, and where that happens it is treated as part of the phenomenon being studied rather than as a bug.

## Why this step is needed after Step 9

Step 9 closed the retrieval-difficulty question but left the domain question open. It showed how the pipeline behaves when the corpus grows 100 times larger, but both corpora were biomedical, so it could say nothing about a genuinely different subject. A method that only works inside the domain it was trained on is far less useful than one whose behaviour is understood when the domain shifts. Step 10 is therefore designed as a hypothesis-driven transfer test rather than a demonstration. Five predictions were written down before the pipeline was run, each one carried in from an earlier step, and the analysis reports which held in the new domain and which did not. Writing the predictions first is what keeps this a test rather than a story fitted to the output afterwards.

## Method

The claim set is 30 real social-media claims, balanced deliberately along three axes so the pipeline is exercised rather than sampled at random: 14 SUPPORT, 7 CONTRADICT and 9 NEI by true label, and 17 near-domain against 13 far-domain by corpus fit. The corpus-fit label is a pre-registered judgement of whether SciFact-Open plausibly contains evidence for the claim, judged by biological mechanism rather than by fish species. Each claim carries its verbatim original quote, a cleaned claim text, the platform it came from with no username retained, and a true label justified against a citable source. The collection and labelling rules are recorded in `collection_guide.md`, and the rules for annotating the pipeline outputs are recorded in `results_annotation_guide.md`, both written before the run.

Each claim was run through three conditions: no retrieval using Model 1, plain dense retrieval into Model 2, and dense retrieval with soft stance reranking into Model 2. The two retrieval conditions were swept over k of 1, 3, 5 and 10, which produces 270 per-claim records in total (30 with no retrieval, 120 plain dense, 120 reranked). The retrieval, reranking and classification components are imported directly from the Step 5 pipeline, so the case study runs the same code path the rest of the thesis evaluates, and the only things that differ are the data and the sweep. The primary comparison for the retrieval-versus-no-retrieval question is fixed in advance at k equal to 3, the SciFact-Open optimum used throughout the project, with the other depths reported as a sensitivity sweep. Accuracy over 30 deliberately chosen claims is treated as an orienting count, not as an estimate of real social-media performance, and confidence throughout means the maximum softmax probability over the three classes, the same definition used in Step 8.

## The five predictions and what happened

The predictions were: H1, that retrieval-related failures involve irrelevant or merely related evidence more for far-fit claims than near-fit ones; H2, that the stance reranker does not rescue out-of-domain retrieval and may reorder it unhelpfully; H3, that on true-CONTRADICT claims the wrong predictions arrive at high confidence, mirroring the Step 8 inversion; H4, that retrieval is no better than, and possibly worse than, no retrieval; and H5, that confident wrong predictions persist.

## H3 is the clearest result: the model is confidently wrong on every false claim

The single most striking pattern in the run is on the CONTRADICT claims. Across all nine condition-and-depth runs (no retrieval, plus dense and dense-plus-rerank at each of four depths), the model scored zero out of seven on the true-CONTRADICT claims. Not one false claim was correctly identified as false in any condition, at any retrieval depth, with or without reranking. At the same time the model was highly confident about these wrong answers. In the no-retrieval condition the mean maximum-softmax probability on the wrong CONTRADICT predictions was 0.85, and under retrieval it rose further, to 0.96 at k of 1 for both dense and reranked. The model is not hesitant about the claims it gets wrong; it is close to maximally certain about them, and it is certain in the same direction every time, predicting SUPPORT or NEI for claims that are actually false.

This is exactly the confidence inversion Step 8 found on SciFact, now reproduced in a completely different domain. That it holds here, on seafood claims against a biomedical corpus, is strong evidence that the inversion is a property of the model and the task rather than an artefact of the SciFact dataset. For a project about when RAG fact-checking fails, this is the central real-world finding: the dangerous failure is not that the model is wrong, but that it is confidently wrong precisely on the false claims, which are the ones a fact-checking system most needs to catch. Measured against the criterion fixed in `results_annotation_guide.md`, that mean confidence on wrong CONTRADICT predictions should exceed the means for wrong SUPPORT and NEI predictions, H3 holds in eight of the nine runs. The exception is the no-retrieval condition, where wrong NEI predictions were marginally more confident than wrong CONTRADICT ones (0.8657 against 0.8524). H5, that confident wrong predictions persist, holds in eight of nine runs by its own pre-registered majority test: 63.1 percent of all 176 incorrect predictions carry a maximum softmax probability of at least 0.80, and only plain dense at k of 10 falls below half, at 44.4 percent. The two exceptions sit at opposite ends of the evidence spectrum, H3 failing only where nothing is retrieved and H5 only where the most is, so the confident-wrong pattern is strongest in the middle of the range a deployed system would actually use.

## H4 is confirmed at the anchor depth but with an important contrast to Step 9

At the pre-registered anchor depth of k equal to 3, retrieval did help. Plain dense reached 30.0 percent and dense with reranking reached 36.7 percent, both above the no-retrieval baseline of 23.3 percent. Read strictly, this means H4 as originally phrased, that retrieval would be no better than no retrieval, does not hold at the anchor depth: retrieval was better. This is a genuine contrast with Step 9, where on the large biomedical corpus no retrieval configuration beat the no-retrieval baseline. The honest reading is that on these out-of-domain claims a small amount of retrieved evidence provided some lift, whereas on in-domain SciFact-Open at scale it did not. Across the full sweep the picture is mixed rather than monotonic: plain dense ranged from 33.3 percent at k of 1 up to 43.3 percent at k of 5, and reranking ranged from 26.7 percent at k of 1 up to 43.3 percent at k of 10. The highest observed accuracy in any retrieval condition was 43.3 percent, but that figure is a post-run maximum across four depths and is reported as exploratory rather than as a selected optimum.

The accuracy numbers should not be over-read. Overall accuracy stays low throughout, between roughly 23 and 43 percent, which is what the domain-transfer framing predicts: a biomedical pipeline meeting seafood claims performs poorly whatever the retrieval depth. The value of Step 10 is not the accuracy figure but the structure of the errors underneath it.

## H2 is confirmed: reranking does not reliably help out of domain

Comparing plain dense against dense with reranking at matched depths shows no consistent benefit from the stance reranker. At k of 1 reranking was worse than plain dense (26.7 against 33.3 percent). At k of 3 it was better (36.7 against 30.0 percent). At k of 5 it was worse again (36.7 against 43.3 percent). At k of 10 it was slightly better (43.3 against 40.0 percent). Averaged across the four depths the two conditions are almost identical, at roughly 36 percent each. This mirrors the Step 4 finding that the reranker does not reliably improve evidence selection, and it extends that finding to a new domain: when the candidate pool is biomedical and the claim is about seafood, promoting the documents that look most stance-bearing does not systematically bring in better evidence, because none of the candidates genuinely bears on the claim. H2 is confirmed.

## H1 needs the manual annotation, and the retrieved documents support it

The by-corpus-fit accuracy in the no-retrieval condition is almost flat, 23.5 percent near against 23.1 percent far, which is expected because no retrieval means corpus fit cannot yet matter. The retrieval conditions are where H1 is tested, and the retrieved document identifiers, printed per claim at the anchor depth, are the raw material for the manual relevance judgement recorded in the annotation table. The pattern visible in the per-claim output is that far-fit claims about certification, ocean policy and fisheries economics, such as the MSC claim, the dolphin-safe claim and the 2048 plastic claim, retrieve biomedical abstracts that have no bearing on the claim at all, whereas some near-fit health claims about omega-3 and mercury retrieve genuinely related cardiovascular or contaminant abstracts. This is the transfer spectrum the claim set was designed to expose, and it supports H1, that irrelevant retrieval dominates the far-fit failures. The full per-claim relevance annotation is carried out against `results_annotation_guide.md` and is the qualitative core of the written chapter.

## What the individual claims show

A few claims illustrate the failure modes concretely. The false claims about parasites, such as parasites surviving cooking and sushi being the leading cause of intestinal parasites, were all predicted as SUPPORT with high confidence, which is the confident-wrong pattern in miniature. The false omega-6 and omega-3 belief claims were predicted as SUPPORT despite being contested or false. Among the claims the pipeline got right were several far-fit SUPPORT claims about aquaculture and sea lice, such as sea lice spreading from farms to wild salmon and escaped farmed salmon threatening wild genetic diversity, which retrieved plausibly related aquaculture abstracts and were classified correctly. The reranked condition changed some individual outcomes, correcting the freezing-kills-parasites claim and the seed-oils claim at k of 3 while breaking the omega-3 cardiovascular claim at the same depth, which is consistent with reranking reordering the evidence without reliably improving it.

## Do the results defend the thesis, and are they valid?

They defend it well. The thesis is a diagnostic account of when and why RAG claim verification fails, and Step 10 shows that the two most important diagnostic findings transfer to a genuinely new domain. The confidence inversion on false claims, first found in Step 8, reappears on real seafood misinformation in eight of the nine runs, which is strong evidence that it is a property of the model rather than of the benchmark. The reranker's failure to reliably help, first found in Step 4, also reappears out of domain. The one finding that does not transfer cleanly is the counterproductive-retrieval result from Step 9, and reporting that honestly, rather than forcing it to match, is itself part of the contribution: retrieval was counterproductive at scale in domain, but provided some lift out of domain, which tells a more precise story than either result alone.

The results are valid within the limits of a qualitative case study. The pipeline is the same code as the earlier steps, the conditions are matched, the corpus fit and true labels were fixed before the run, the primary depth was pre-registered, the confidence definition matches Step 8, and the run provenance including a hash of the claim file is saved with the output. The accuracy figures are treated as orienting counts over 30 claims, not as performance estimates.

## Limitations to state plainly

The claim set is 30 claims, so no accuracy figure here is a population estimate, and it is not presented as one. The true labels rest on reviewer judgement against cited sources, and for a few claims, such as the wild-versus-farmed omega-3 claim, the label is genuinely close to the line and the nuance is recorded in the claim file. The manual relevance annotation is a single-annotator judgement rather than a doubly-annotated one, which is a reasonable scope decision for a solo project but is weaker than the Step 7 protocol. The reranker effect at k of 10 can only reorder rather than filter, because the candidate pool equals the retrieval depth at that setting, so any change there is attributable to ordering rather than to inclusion or exclusion, and this is marked in the annotation. Finally, the claims come from real people, and although usernames are not retained, a verbatim quote can sometimes be traced back, so the data are described as pseudonymised rather than fully anonymous.

## Relevance to the thesis as a whole

Step 10 is the final rung. Steps 2 to 8 built and probed the pipeline in domain, Step 9 tested it as retrieval scaled, and Step 10 tests it as the domain changes. Together they support the single claim the project makes: that retrieval-augmented claim verification fails in structured, diagnosable ways, that the most dangerous of those failures is confident wrongness on false claims, and that this failure is stable across corpus scale and across domain. Step 10 is where that claim meets real-world misinformation and holds.

## Files

The claim set is `realworld/seafood_claims.csv`. The case-study script is `realworld/seafood_claims.py`. The collection and labelling rules are `realworld/collection_guide.md`, and the output-annotation protocol is `realworld/results_annotation_guide.md`. The run outputs are `results/step10_realworld/step10_records.json`, holding all 270 per-claim records, and `results/step10_realworld/step10_summary.json`, holding the per-condition accuracy, the by-corpus-fit split, the confidence-by-true-label tables, the H4 anchor comparison and the run provenance.

## Reproduction

Run on a GPU, which encodes the SciFact-Open corpus once in roughly 40 minutes:

#running the Step 10 case study across all three conditions and all four depths
python realworld/seafood_claims.py --claims_csv realworld/seafood_claims.csv --model1_path models/saved_models/baseline_scifact --model2_path models/saved_models/evidence_scifact --out_dir results/step10_realworld --k_values 1 3 5 10

The run prints a per-condition read-out and saves the two JSON files above. A correct run reports 270 saved records. The qualitative analysis is then carried out by hand against the annotation guide.
